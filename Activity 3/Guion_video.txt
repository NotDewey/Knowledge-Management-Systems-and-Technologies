When a support problem comes up, finding a previous solution can take longer than expected. An analyst may remember that a similar incident happened, but not know where the procedure was saved. A document recommender helps by bringing related information into one list.

For this activity, I used Nexo IT Services, a fictional support company, and a collection of thirty simulated documents. The collection includes policies, incident records and training material. The system accepts a document identifier or a question and returns five related documents.

The method is TF-IDF with cosine similarity. It gives weight to words and phrases that help distinguish one document from another, then compares their vectors. The scores show how similar the wording is. They do not tell us whether an instruction is correct or whether someone is authorized to follow it.

In the file recovery test, the restoration procedure, DOC007, appeared first. The recovery FAQ and a restoration exercise appeared after it. This gives an analyst a useful starting point instead of having to recreate a recovery plan from memory. The analyst can open the procedure, check the conditions and confirm who must approve the restoration.

This is how recommendations could reduce rework and help decisions happen sooner. They connect a new problem with knowledge that already exists. I did not measure the time saved by real staff, so I would treat that benefit as something to test with users.

The main limitation is that similar wording does not always mean similar purpose. A VPN workshop can share words with a recovery query even though it does not explain how to fix the connection. The opposite can happen with synonyms. A useful article might say corporate tunnel instead of VPN and receive a lower score.

Ambiguous words can connect unrelated topics. Short documents may not contain enough detail, while long documents can dilute an important instruction among other information. The collection is also small and simulated, so it reflects the examples chosen for this exercise rather than the full range of a real service desk.

For a real organization, I would add the document owner, purpose, approval status, version and review date to the ranking process. For an incident question, a current approved procedure should have priority over general training. Access filters would need to run on a server so that employees only receive documents they are allowed to see.

I would also compare a synonym list or semantic search with this baseline. The comparison should use new queries and relevance labels reviewed by other people. Repeated evaluation and document updates would show whether the changes actually help.

The recommender is useful when it makes the right source easier to find. The analyst still needs to read that source and decide whether it applies to the situation.
